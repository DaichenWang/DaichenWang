# 01_cover

Good morning. I'm Daichen Wang, a doctoral candidate at Queensland University of Technology. The project is called Root-Seeking, or Xun Gen. Xun gen means root-seeking — the search for where you come from. What's on screen is not a photograph. It's our virtual reconstruction of a classical Chinese garden, built for people who can no longer easily go back to one.

---

# 02_motivation

I want to start with the moment this research actually began, because it explains the whole thing better than an abstract can. On the ninth of February this year I was standing in the waiting hall of Wuxi Railway Station. It was the peak of the Lunar New Year rush. Everyone around me was going home, and in a few days I would be flying back to Brisbane. That is the tension the project sits inside. For some people return is simply a rhythm of life. For others it has become a periodic exception, something you plan and save for and eventually stop planning. The Chinese diaspora numbers in the tens of millions, and for a great many of them the desire to reconnect with where they came from stays powerful and persistent long after returning stops being practical.

---

# 03_roots_to_routes

So the first question is what kind of thing that desire actually is. Fei Xiaotong described traditional Chinese society as Xiangtu, soil-bound: identity, kinship and obligation inseparable from the land you were born on, organised in concentric circles he called Chaxu Geju. Chunyun is its most visible modern expression. But Fei's frame reaches a limit with permanent migrants, whose geographical centre has already moved and is not moving back. So the research shifts from roots to routes, and turns to Avtar Brah. Homing desire, she argues, is not the desire to return to a particular place. Home is a mythic place of desire in the diasporic imagination: a feeling you construct, not a location you reach. And Paul Connerton tells us where that construction happens. Cultural memory is not only in texts and monuments; it is inscribed in the body through habit. Familiar spaces, familiar sounds, familiar ways of moving are the mechanism.

---

# 04_research_gap

Which brings me to what has not been built. Despite a lot of very good XR heritage research, there is no peer-reviewed, empirically validated social VR environment designed specifically for the cultural and spatial needs of the Chinese diaspora. The tools people already use — WeChat, video calls, social media — do sustain relationships across distance, and I want to be fair to them. What they cannot do is reproduce the embodied spatial familiarity Fei identified as the bedrock of that soil-bound world. You can see your mother's face on a screen; you cannot walk through the courtyard behind her. On the research side, the preliminary VR-for-diaspora studies either lacked cultural specificity or narrowed to student populations, and none of them centres the classical Chinese garden as a carrier for root-seeking.

---

# 05_questions

The doctoral inquiry sits at two levels. The overarching question is bigger than any single platform: when permanent physical return is no longer viable, how can the sensory, spatial and social conditions of cultural belonging be reconstituted elsewhere? I operationalise that through one designed case, and I want to be precise about the word case. The garden is an instrumental case, not the object of the thesis. Underneath it sit three subsidiary questions. Which cultural, spatial and sensory elements do diaspora members themselves identify as essential. How participatory co-design can genuinely engage a diaspora community in shaping something culturally authentic. And how participants actually experience the environment in terms of belonging and emotional reconnection.

---

# 06_three_phases

The study answers those in three phases. Phase One was survey work and building. A global online survey of the Chinese diaspora returned two hundred and six questionnaires across two rounds, of which a hundred and seventy-two were retained. On site at Jichang Garden, fifty visitors and staff, twenty-seven questionnaires — fifteen visitors, twelve administrators. Phase Two took the prototype to Brisbane: twelve participants in two groups of six. I recruited through Chinese-language social media, the Students and Scholars Association, and deliberately through a local table tennis club, because I did not want a room full of students. Phase Three was folded into those same sessions: pre- and post-experience state questionnaires plus a twenty-six item experience measure covering interface, immersion, cultural recognition and social presence. All of it under QUT ethics approval.

# 07_fieldwork

Before the numbers, the rooms they came from. On the left, Jichang Garden — the team on site with visitors and staff. Fifty people engaged with us there, twenty-seven filled in the written questionnaire, and the demonstrations that followed reached forty. On the right, Brisbane in June: participants inside the VR environment on a Quest 3, across the two focus groups. The same prototype, two very different rooms. In one, the garden is somewhere people walk through on a Sunday afternoon. In the other, it is the thing they left behind.

# 08_evidence

So what did those rooms tell us. On the left, the on-site visitor items, ranked. Gardens being important in Chinese culture sits highest at four point four seven, ninety-three per cent agreement. Willingness to visit one abroad and gardens relieving pressure both sit at four point three three. But look at the bottom two bars, in the lighter blue. I feel emotionally attached drops to three point eight, and it holds special significance for me drops to three point five three, with fewer than half agreeing in each case. Recognition is running ahead of attachment, in the same people at the same sitting. On the right, the online survey set the requirements. Seasonal change and the passage of time rated highest of the five cultural authenticity items, cultural symbols second. And the clearest result was negative: a hundred and thirty of a hundred and seventy-two named overly complex operation as the thing to avoid. That pushed the prototype away from gamification and towards ritual.

# 09_two_platforms

So two platforms were built, and they are one designed environment rather than two projects. On the left, the high-fidelity world: Unity 2022.3 through VRChat for Meta Quest 3, ninety frames per second in a three by three metre volume, with a desktop fallback so nobody is excluded for lack of a headset. On the right, Minecraft Java Edition on a custom server with an AI cultural guide through MinePal — configured rather than engineered: I wrote it a character brief covering classical poetry, Daoist naturalism and garden design, rather than training anything. The line at the top is the important one. Minecraft is the methodological engine of Phase Two, not a demo. Its coarse blocks keep building visible and reversible for people with no 3D modelling skills, and its message boards and personal courtyards let them contribute when they are not in the room.

---

# 10_current_status

Where it stands today. Data collection is complete across all three phases. The VR environment is fully operational, holding ninety frames per second on Quest 3, and it has been publicly exhibited. The Minecraft platform with the MinePal guide is complete, with over ninety per cent conversational accuracy across roughly twenty test rounds. A hundred and two artifacts scanned from Jichang Garden — using handheld iPhone LiDAR, Gaussian Splatting and aerial photogrammetry — are openly available on Sketchfab. The one thing still in progress is the thematic analysis of the June focus-group data, and a third iteration in Unreal Engine.

---

# 11_contributions

The work makes three contributions, and they are addressed to three different rooms. To diaspora and memory studies, a theoretical one: a model of root-seeking as sensory and embodied memory activation, which is what happens when you put Fei's Xiangtu in dialogue with Brah's homing desire. To digital heritage and human-computer interaction, an empirical one: to my knowledge the first peer-reviewed, empirically evaluated social VR environment tailored to Chinese diaspora cultural needs. And to community-centred design practice, a methodological one: a co-design model for culturally specific XR that other people can run. The line underneath matters for how you should read all of this. The empirical findings are situated in this case. The theoretical model and the methodology are the parts designed to travel.

---

# 12_next_steps

Four things next. Finish the thematic analysis, which drives every remaining update to both platforms. Compare the two modalities by triangulating the questionnaire patterns against the discussion themes, because embodied presence and social building may well shape root-seeking differently. Finish the Unreal Engine version and then compare across all three. And bring motion-captured classical dance into both worlds with the QUT XR Screen Futures Hub, so that root-seeking extends past exploring a space into performing in it. Let me leave you with the claim I would most like challenged. If root-seeking operates through sensory memory rather than physical location, then the question is not where it can happen. It is how. Thank you.
