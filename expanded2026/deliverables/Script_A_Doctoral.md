# 01_cover

Good morning. I'm Daichen Wang, a doctoral candidate at Queensland University of Technology. The project is called Root-Seeking, or Xun Gen. Xun gen means root-seeking — the search for where you come from. What's on screen is not a photograph. It's our virtual reconstruction of a classical Chinese garden, built for people who can no longer easily go back to one.

---

# 02_motivation

I want to start with the moment this project really began. On the ninth of February this year I was standing in the waiting hall of Wuxi Railway Station. It was the busiest week of the Lunar New Year. Everyone around me was going home. In a few days I would be flying back to Brisbane. That gap is where the project sits. For some people, going home is just part of the year. For others it turns into a trip you save up for, and then one you stop planning at all. There are tens of millions of Chinese people living overseas, and for many of them the wish to reconnect with where they came from stays strong long after going back stops being easy.

---

# 03_roots_to_routes

So what kind of feeling is that? Fei Xiaotong described old Chinese society as tied to the soil. He called it Xiangtu. Who you are, who your family is, what you owe people — all of it grew out of the land you were born on, in circles spreading out from you. But that model runs out with people who move away for good, because their centre has already moved, and it is not moving back. So we shift from roots to routes, and turn to Avtar Brah. She calls it a homing desire. It is not the wish to go back to one place. Home, she says, is a place you build in your head, not a place you arrive at. And Paul Connerton tells us where you build it. Culture is not only kept in books and monuments. It is kept in the body, in habit. Familiar spaces, familiar sounds, familiar ways of moving — that is the machinery.

---

# 04_research_gap

Which brings me to what has not been built. There is a lot of good work on XR and heritage, but no social VR space has been peer-reviewed, tested with real users, and designed around what Chinese people living overseas need, in culture and in space. The tools they already use — WeChat, video calls, social media — do keep relationships alive across distance. What they cannot give you is the feeling of being in a familiar place, the thing Fei said held that world together. You can see your mother's face on a screen. You cannot walk through the courtyard behind her. And the few VR studies that do look at this either skip the cultural detail or only test students, and none of them puts the classical Chinese garden at the centre.

---

# 05_questions

The research works on two levels. The big question is bigger than any one platform: when going back for good is no longer realistic, how do you rebuild the feel of belonging somewhere else — the senses, the space, the people? I answer that by building one case and studying it closely. And I want to be careful with the word case. The garden is a tool for asking the question. It is not the point of the thesis. Under it sit three smaller questions. Which parts of the culture, the space and the senses do people themselves say matter most. How do you get a community properly involved in shaping something that feels real to them. And how do people actually feel inside it.

---

# 06_three_phases

There are three phases. Phase one was a survey plus building. An online survey of Chinese people living overseas brought back two hundred and six responses over two rounds, and we kept a hundred and seventy-two. On site at Jichang Garden we talked with fifty visitors and staff, and twenty-seven filled in a form — fifteen visitors, twelve staff. Phase two took the prototype to Brisbane: twelve people, in two groups of six. I found them through Chinese social media, through the students and scholars association, and on purpose through a local table tennis club, because I did not want a room full of students. Phase three sat inside those same sessions: a short form before and after, plus a twenty-six question survey on the interface, the sense of being there, cultural recognition, and being with other people. All of it under QUT ethics approval.

# 07_fieldwork

Before the numbers, here are the rooms they came from. On the left, Jichang Garden, with our team on site talking to visitors and staff. Fifty people took part, twenty-seven filled in the written form, and the demonstrations after that reached forty. On the right, Brisbane in June: people inside the VR world on a Quest 3, across the two groups. Same prototype, two very different rooms. In one, the garden is somewhere you walk on a Sunday afternoon. In the other, it is the thing you left behind.

# 08_evidence

So what did those rooms tell us. On the left are the visitor answers from the garden, ranked. Gardens matter in Chinese culture is at the top, four point four seven, with ninety-three per cent agreeing. Would visit one abroad, and gardens help you relax, both sit at four point three three. But look at the bottom two bars, the lighter blue ones. I feel emotionally attached drops to three point eight. It means something special to me drops to three point five three, and fewer than half agreed with either. People know the garden matters, but they do not feel close to it — the same people, in the same sitting. On the right, the online survey told us what to build. Seasonal change and the passing of time came first, symbols second. And the clearest answer was a negative one: a hundred and thirty people out of a hundred and seventy-two said the thing to avoid is making it too complicated. That pushed the design away from games and towards ritual.

---

# 09_two_platforms

So we built two platforms, and they are one design rather than two projects. On the left, the high-detail world: Unity 2022.3, running through VRChat on Meta Quest 3, ninety frames a second in a three by three metre space, with a desktop version so nobody is left out for not owning a headset. On the right, Minecraft Java Edition on our own server, with an AI cultural guide through a service called MinePal. I want to say configured, not built: I wrote it a character brief — classical poetry, Daoist ideas about nature, garden design — I did not train a model. The line at the top is the important one. Minecraft is how phase two works, not a demo. Its rough blocks keep building easy to see and easy to undo for people who have never touched 3D software, and its message boards let people add things when they are not in the room.

---

# 10_current_status

Where things stand today. All three phases of data collection are done. The VR world is fully working, holding ninety frames a second on Quest 3, and it has been shown in public. The Minecraft platform with the MinePal guide is finished, with over ninety per cent accurate answers across about twenty test rounds. A hundred and two objects scanned at Jichang Garden — with a phone LiDAR scanner, with Gaussian Splatting, and with drone photos — are free to look at on Sketchfab. What is still open is the analysis of the June focus group talks, and a third version in Unreal Engine.

---

# 11_contributions

Three contributions, and each one speaks to a different room. To people who study migration and memory, a theory: root-seeking as memory brought back through the body and the senses, which is what you get when you put Fei's soil-bound world next to Brah's homing desire. To digital heritage and human-computer interaction, evidence: as far as I know, the first peer-reviewed social VR space built and tested for what Chinese people living overseas need. And to community-centred design, a method other people can pick up and run. The line underneath matters for how you read all of it. The findings belong to this case. The theory and the method are the parts meant to travel.

# 12_next_steps

Four things next. Finish the analysis, which feeds every remaining change to both platforms. Compare the two platforms by putting the survey patterns next to what people said in the group talks, because being there and building together may work on people in different ways. Finish the Unreal Engine version, then compare all three. And bring motion-captured classical dance into both worlds with the QUT XR Screen Futures Hub, so root-seeking is not only walking through a place but performing in it. Let me finish with the claim I would most like you to push back on. If root-seeking works through the senses and through memory rather than through being in a physical place, then the question is not where it can happen. It is how. Thank you.
